Inside the Black Box

A plain-language guide to mechanistic interpretability: the science of reverse-engineering neural networks. It goes from the first saliency maps in 2013 to attribution graphs of frontier language models today.

The story in five sentences

  • Neural networks learn their own algorithms during training, and nobody writes those algorithms down. Mechanistic interpretability tries to reverse engineer them.
  • Researchers first looked inside vision models (2013–2021). They found curve detectors, dog-head detectors and real circuits, read straight from the weights.
  • They also found that many neurons mix unrelated concepts, because networks pack more features than they have neurons (superposition). In transformers, they tested explanations with causal tools such as activation patching.
  • Sparse autoencoders (2023–2024) unpacked superposition into millions of readable features, and attribution graphs (2025) traced how Claude plans rhymes, does arithmetic and refuses requests.
  • Since 2025 the field has turned practical: white-box audits in model system cards, cheap probes in production, models that explain their own activations in words, and a debate on whether to aim for full understanding or for simple tools that work.

Four eras of the field

2013 – 2019

Seeing inside vision models

Saliency maps, feature visualization, DeepDream, Network Dissection, TCAV, and the first readable neurons in text models.

2020 – 2021

The Circuits program

"Zoom In", curve detectors, multimodal neurons, the name "superposition", and the first transformer math framework.

2022 – 2023

Circuits in language models

Induction heads, IOI, grokking, superposition toy models, activation patching, steering vectors, the first sparse autoencoders.

2024 – 2026

Scale, use and a pragmatic turn

SAEs on frontier models (Golden Gate Claude), attribution graphs, white-box audits in system cards (from Sept 2025), probes in production (Jan 2026), natural language autoencoders (2026), and "method minimalism": test against simple baselines first.

A note on sources

This survey summarizes published papers and research posts. Where we give a number or a model name, it comes from the original source, which is linked. Simple language means we must sometimes leave out detail. When in doubt, read the paper.
