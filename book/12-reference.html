How the ideas connect

The field did not grow in a straight line. Some ideas motivated others: polysemantic neurons motivated the study of superposition, and superposition motivated sparse autoencoders. Some ideas built on others: transcoders built on SAEs, and attribution graphs built on transcoders. Some methods test the claims of others: patching tests circuit hypotheses.

The map below shows these links. Columns go roughly from older ideas (left) to newer ideas (right). The last column holds what became important in 2026: white-box audits in system cards, natural language autoencoders, probes in production, and personas. Four threads to follow

The "what" thread (top of the map) asks what a model represents: feature visualization → polysemanticity → superposition → sparse autoencoders → self-explaining models → natural language autoencoders.

The "how" thread (bottom) asks how parts compute: residual stream → attention heads → induction heads → hand-found circuits → attribution graphs.

The "use" thread (right) asks what the tools are good for: directions and probes → probes in production; auditing games → white-box audits of real models before release.

The "prove it" thread (bottom left) asks which parts cause behaviour: ablation → activation patching → path patching → causal scrubbing, ACDC and DAS. The strongest results use several threads at once.

Five eras stand out:

  1. Roots (before 2013). Mathematics that later explained superposition (the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, compressed sensing) and sparse coding of images.
  2. Seeing inside vision models (2013–2019). Saliency maps, feature visualization, Network Dissection and TCAV. People asked "what does this unit detect?"
  3. Circuits (2020–2021). The Distill Circuits thread and the first mathematical framework for transformers. People asked "how do units connect?"
  4. LM circuits and superposition (2022–2023). Induction heads, IOI, grokking, toy models of superposition, patching methods and the first SAEs.
  5. Scale, replacement models and use (2024–2026). SAEs on frontier models, attribution graphs, self-explaining models (up to natural language autoencoders), probes in production, and white-box audits of real models before release.

Test yourself

These questions cover the whole survey. If you get one wrong, the explanation points to the chapter to reread.

Why do many neurons respond to several unrelated concepts?
Superposition lets a model pack many sparse features into fewer dimensions. Neurons then mix features. See Chapter 3.
An induction head predicts [B] after seeing [A][B] … [A]. Which earlier head does it need?
Induction heads use K-composition with a previous-token head. See Chapter 4.
In activation patching, you run the corrupted prompt and copy in one clean activation. The output becomes almost clean. What does that show?
This is denoising. It tests sufficiency. Noising (the opposite direction) tests necessity. See Chapter 5.
A linear probe reads "is this statement true?" from layer 12 with high accuracy. What should you do next to show the model uses this information?
Probes show presence. Interventions show use. See Chapter 7.
What is the main trade-off when you train a sparse autoencoder?
A stronger sparsity penalty gives fewer active features but worse reconstruction. See Chapter 8.
Why did Anthropic use cross-layer transcoders instead of plain SAEs to build attribution graphs?
SAE features sit on activations, but the MLP between them is still a black box. Transcoders replace that computation. See Chapter 9.
In the "Biology of a Large Language Model" poem example, what showed that Claude plans ahead?
The plan features appeared at the newline before the line. Suppressing or replacing them changed how the line ended. See Chapter 9.
For "the capital of the state containing Dallas is", the attribution graph shows Dallas → Texas → say "Austin". What happened when researchers suppressed the "Texas" features?
Without "Texas", the model no longer knew which capital to say, so it spread its bets over generic words. Other capitals (Albany, Sacramento) appeared only when the "Dallas" features were suppressed instead. Swapping "Texas" for "California" gave "Sacramento". See Chapter 9.
Which statement best describes the state of the field in 2026?
The field has real tools and real uses (white-box audits in system cards, probes in production), and also open problems and active debate (attribution graphs explain only some prompts; probes can be evaded; SAE versus simpler methods). See Chapter 11.

About this survey

How it was made

  • Research notes. The survey is built on seven research notes, one per topic (history, superposition, transformer circuits, probing and steering, dictionary learning, causal methods and the frontier, researchers and open gaps), plus an eighth recency sweep of June 2025 to October 2026. They are in the notes/ folder next to this site. Each note lists its sources and tags claims as verified, secondary or [UNSURE]. The chapters leave out or soften claims that could not be checked.
  • Independent fact-check. In October 2026, every chapter got a separate fact-check pass. Reviewers pulled out the checkable claims (names, author lists, dates, numbers, quotes, head and neuron IDs, URLs), compared them with the primary sources (papers, arXiv records, lab posts, system-card PDFs), fixed errors, and tested every external link. Each pass wrote a changelog (old text, new text, reason, source) in notes/review/. This reference chapter, the glossary and the timeline were then checked against the corrected chapters.
  • Simple language. The text follows the spirit of ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English: short sentences, active voice, one idea per sentence, common words, and one meaning per term. Technical terms are defined at first use and in the glossary.
  • Real data where possible. The activation-patching heatmaps in Chapter 5 are real results. We computed them on GPT-2 small for the IOI prompt "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to", with the script book/scripts/patch_ioi.py. They reproduce the published IOI story: S-inhibition heads 7.3, 7.9, 8.6 and 8.10, name movers 9.6, 9.9 and 10.0, and negative heads 10.7 and 11.10. The Chapter 9 explorer shows the output numbers reported in the "Biology" paper's intervention figures (for example, suppressing "Texas" drops "Austin" from 98% to 6% with no other capital taking over).
  • Illustrations. Some widgets use toy models that train live in your browser (superposition in Chapter 3, SAEs in Chapter 8). Others are schematic and say so in their captions. They show the shape of a published result, not new data.

Limits

  • This survey is current as of October 2026. The timeline ends in September 2026. The field moves fast, and newer results may change some conclusions.
  • Some 2025–2026 results come from lab blog posts and system cards, not peer-reviewed papers. The chapters say so, and they leave out numbers that only appeared in secondary reports.
  • Simple language leaves out detail. For any exact number or claim, read the original paper. Every chapter ends with its references.
  • Coverage is broad, not complete. Some threads (for example, interpretability of speech models or of robotics policies) get little or no space.
Where to go from here

The best way to learn mechanistic interpretability is to do it. Pick a small open model, load it with TransformerLens or nnsight, and reproduce one result from this survey, such as induction heads or the IOI patching map. Then change one thing and see what happens.
