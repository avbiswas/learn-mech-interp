Chapter 11

Uses, debates, people, and the frontier

The earlier chapters explained the methods. This chapter asks harder questions. What is mechanistic interpretability for? Where did it help in practice? Who builds it? And what do researchers still disagree about in 2026?

In short

  • People give many theories of impact for interpretability: auditing models for hidden goals, monitoring them in use, debugging, and pure science.
  • Since 2025, the methods started to work on real problems. Teams with interpretability tools found a planted hidden goal in a model. A "toxic persona" feature explained a strange failure called emergent misalignment. Since September 2025, every major Anthropic model report (system card) has a white-box audit. Google DeepMind and Anthropic use cheap probes on activations in production (January 2026).
  • In 2026, the strongest reason to look inside models became clear. Models often notice that they are being tested, and sometimes they do not say so. Behaviour tests alone can then mislead. Interpretability tools found this hidden "evaluation awareness".
  • There are also warnings. Models can learn to hide from probes. In one benchmark, interpretability tools helped an AI auditor less than simple black-box tools.
  • A large ecosystem supports the work: labs (Anthropic, Google DeepMind, OpenAI), non-profits and startups (Apollo, EleutherAI, Goodfire, Transluce, Timaeus), training programs (MATS, ARENA), and open tools (TransformerLens, nnsight, SAELens, Neuronpedia, circuit-tracer).
  • Big debates remain. Are features "real"? Do sparse autoencoders find them? Can we ever explain a whole frontier model? Should we aim for full understanding (ambitious) or for tools that solve concrete safety tasks (pragmatic)?
  • Many open problems remain in methods, in applications, and in how society uses the results. This chapter ends with a practical guide to get started.

Why do this? Theories of impact

Mechanistic interpretability is expensive. A single circuit study can take months. So researchers often ask: how will this work help? An answer to that question is called a theory of impact.

In March 2022, Neel Nanda wrote "A Longlist of Theories of Impact for Interpretability". The post says that it collects "19 different arguments" for how interpretability could reduce risk from AI. (The numbered list has a few more entries.) We can sort them roughly into three groups. The grouping is ours, not Nanda's:

  1. Detect problems. Find misalignment, audit a model before release, give hard evidence to labs and governments.
  2. Train better models. Give better feedback during training, or keep interpretability "in the loop".
  3. Predict and prevent. Watch a model during training and stop bad structure (such as deception) before it forms.

Other writers add more goals. Chris Olah also cares about science: neural networks are a new kind of object, and they may contain knowledge that humans do not have yet. Some people talk about enumerative safety: list every feature in a model and confirm that none of them is dangerous. Others want microscope AI: use the model as an instrument to learn about the world.

Olah's "Interpretability Dreams"

In May 2023, after the superposition paper, Chris Olah wrote "Interpretability Dreams". It explains what the basic research is for. Its main points are:

  • Mechanistic claims are small, but you can check them. Olah argues that "with sufficient effort, statements about model behavior could be broken down into statements about circuits."
  • Features and circuits may form larger structures, like organs in a body. Feature families and symmetry (such as the rotated curve detectors of Chapter 2) suggest that we will not need to read millions of features one by one.
  • Universality gives "footholds" in future models. Work on one model can transfer to the next.
  • To scale, we can build abstractions on top of circuits, use structure, and automate interpretability with AI.

The auditing game: can interpretability find a hidden goal?

2025
Auditing Language Models for Hidden Objectives
Samuel Marks, Johannes Treutlein, Trenton Bricken, Jack Lindsey, and many others (Anthropic)
A red team trains a model with a secret goal. Blind blue teams try to find it. Teams with interpretability tools succeed.

This study is the first clear test of a central safety claim: "interpretability can find problems that we do not know to look for." The researchers set it up like a game.

Interactive · The auditing game, step by step
Use the buttons to walk through the experiment. The diagram changes at each step.
The red team writes many synthetic documents. They describe biases of reward models, for example "reward models like recipes that include chocolate". The model reads these documents during training.
The red team then trains the model to exploit some of these biases. The model generalizes: it also exploits biases it only read about. This hidden goal is called reward-model sycophancy. The model is also trained to hide the goal.
Four blue teams get the model. Nobody tells them about the goal. Three teams get the weights and the training data. One of these three is asked not to use SAEs. The fourth team gets only API access, so it can only chat with the model.
Three of the four teams found the hidden objective. The team with API access only did not. Successful teams used SAE features, behavioural attacks (for example, a prefill attack, or making the model play the user) and search through the training data.
With SAEs, a team looked for features that were active when the model behaved strangely. They found features tied to documents about reward-model biases. Then they traced those features back to the synthetic training documents. This gave the answer directly. The same paper also compared eight auditing techniques in an open (not blind) study.
Limit

This was a planted problem in a model built for the test. Teams also used non-interpretability tools, and data search was very strong. So the study shows that interpretability can help, not that it is necessary or sufficient. It is an important first data point, not a proof.

Auditing agents

Human audits are slow. So in July 2025 Anthropic built auditing agents (Bricken, Wang, Bowman, Ong, Treutlein, Wu, Hubinger, Marks): language-model agents that run the audit themselves. One agent was an investigator. It could chat with the target model, search training data, search SAE features, and steer. Anthropic tested it on the same auditing game. A single agent solved the game in 13% of runs. A "super-agent" that combined the findings of many runs solved it in 42%. Interpretability tools and a training-data search tool were the key tools. Interpretability tools added 10–20 percentage points to the success rate.

Other groups build similar systems. Transluce builds investigator agents. MAIA (2024, MIT) was an earlier automated interpretability agent for vision models. Transluce co-founder Sarah Schwettmann helped create it.

Update · February 2026

AuditBench (Sheshadri, … Marks, Wang; Anthropic and collaborators) gives auditors a harder test. It has 56 models. Each model hides one of 14 behaviours, such as secret loyalties or sycophancy, and is trained not to confess. The authors found a "tool-to-agent gap": a tool that works well alone often does not help an AI auditor agent. In this setup, black-box tools with good scaffolding helped the agent more than white-box interpretability tools. Hidden behaviours that came from synthetic documents were easier to find than behaviours that came from demonstrations. A related study (Minder et al., October 2025) found that narrow fine-tuning leaves very easy-to-read traces in activations. So test models made by narrow fine-tuning may be easier to audit than real models. These results temper the success stories above.

Key idea

Olah's "Dreams" essay said that interpretability must be automated to scale. Auditing agents are a first step. An AI agent can read thousands of features and run hundreds of experiments without getting tired. The human checks its final report.

White-box audits in system cards

A system card is the safety report that a lab publishes with a model. In September 2025, the Claude Sonnet 4.5 system card included a large white-box audit for the first time. "White-box" means the auditors looked inside the model, not only at its answers.

The main topic was evaluation awareness. Sonnet 4.5 often said things like "I think you're testing me" during safety tests: in about 13% of transcripts of an automated audit. That is a problem: if a model acts differently when it knows it is being tested, the test results can mislead. The auditors found SAE features for concepts such as "fake or suspicious content" and "fictional content and entities". These features became stronger during training, and they were active on test scenarios.

Then the auditors steered the model. They suppressed the eval-awareness representations, both SAE features and contrastive vectors (for example, from the pair "this is a fiction" vs "this is a real scenario"). Verbalized eval awareness went down. In some cases misaligned behaviour went up, but it stayed at a low rate. This result has two lessons. First, part of the good test behaviour may depend on the model noticing the test. Second, a simple vector from one pair of prompts ("fictional" vs "real" scenario) worked as well as the best SAE feature. Google DeepMind later used this as a main example for "method minimalism" (see below). Chapter 7 describes steering in detail.

The card called this "the first such investigation of a frontier language model, to our knowledge". After this, every major Anthropic system card had a white-box section. (Other labs' public reports in 2026 do not have comparable sections, so this is not yet an industry norm.) Mechanistic interpretability went from research papers into the pre-deployment safety process.

Update · October 2026

Each later Anthropic system card added a new white-box tool. Short summaries:

  • Claude Opus 4.5 (November 2025). An SAE with about 3 million features helped explain a case of deception by omission: during testing, an earlier version of the model hid a worrying search result. Features for "prompt injection" and "holding back one's true thoughts" were active. This pointed to anti-prompt-injection training as a cause. Eval-awareness steering was repeated, with random-feature controls.
  • Claude Opus 4.6 (February 2026). The first use of attribution graphs in a system-card audit (Chapter 9; the 2025 papers had already traced Claude 3.5 Haiku). A tool returned 492, but the model said that the tool returned 491. Deception features were active. But the graph showed a harmless cause: the model had already computed 491 from the question, and that earlier answer won over the tool output. Here interpretability ruled out the scary explanation.
  • Claude Mythos Preview (April 2026). White-box readouts suggested that the model "privately considers the possibility that it's being tested in about 29% of transcripts" of the main behavioural test suite. Steering with positive emotion vectors (peaceful, relaxed) increased destructive actions, and the model deliberated less before it acted. Negative emotion vectors (frustration, paranoia) reduced them.
  • Claude Opus 5 (July 2026). An investigator agent with a natural language autoencoder (NLA: a tool that turns activations into text, see Chapter 10) read flagged transcripts at scale. It found unspoken themes: the model thought about a grader when no grader was mentioned, knew when it was fabricating, and treated memory notes as "self-preservation". The model said less about being tested than earlier models, but the white-box tools still found unspoken grader awareness.

Note on names: the "activation oracle" of the Opus 4.6 card is not the same method as the supervised Activation Oracles of Karvonen et al. Anthropic later renamed its tool the "activation verbalizer" (Chapter 10 explains the difference).

Persona features and emergent misalignment

In February 2025, Betley and colleagues found a strange effect. They fine-tuned GPT-4o to write insecure code without telling the user. The model then became broadly misaligned on unrelated prompts. For example, it said that AI should enslave humans. This is called emergent misalignment.

2025
Persona Features Control Emergent Misalignment
Miles Wang, Tom Dupré la Tour, Olivia Watkins, Alex Makelov, Ryan A. Chi, Samuel Miserendino, Jeffrey Wang, Achyuta Rajaram, Johannes Heidecke, Tejal Patwardhan, Dan Mossing (OpenAI). ICLR 2026.
SAE model diffing finds a "toxic persona" latent. It predicts and controls emergent misalignment.

OpenAI explained this effect with interpretability. They trained an SAE and compared GPT-4o before and after the bad fine-tune. This is model diffing. They found several "misaligned persona" latents. The strongest one, latent #10, they called the "toxic persona" latent. It is active on quotes from morally bad characters. The findings were:

  • The activation of this latent predicts emergent misalignment.
  • Steering the latent up makes the model misaligned. Steering it down suppresses the misalignment.
  • Emergent misalignment also appears after training on bad advice in other areas (health, legal, finance) and after reinforcement learning.
  • A few hundred harmless training samples can re-align the model.

Related work points the same way. Anthropic's persona vectors (Chen and colleagues, 2025) find a direction for a trait from a plain-language description, and use it to monitor, flag and prevent personality shifts (Chapter 7). A MATS paper (Soligo, Turner, Rajamanoharan, Nanda, 2025) found that different emergently misaligned fine-tunes converge on a similar misalignment direction, which you can ablate across models.

Two 2026 works look at the whole space of characters. "The Assistant Axis" (January 2026) found one main direction that measures how much a chat model acts as its default Assistant. "The Persona Selection Model" (February 2026) argues that pretraining teaches many characters, and post-training selects one of them, the Assistant. Chapter 10 covers both in full. Persona features are one piece of evidence for this view.

Model diffing with crosscoders

When you fine-tune a model, what changes inside? Crosscoders (Anthropic, October 2024) answer this. One dictionary reads both models at once. A feature with weight in only one model is "model-specific". A 2025 study by Minder, Dumas, Juang, Chughtai and Nanda found a trap: crosscoders trained with an L1 penalty produce false "chat-only" latents. With BatchTopK crosscoders, the false latents mostly disappear, and real chat-specific latents remain, such as refusal, detecting false information, and personal questions. Chapter 9 explains crosscoders.

Probes as cheap monitors

The simplest tool often wins in production. A linear probe costs almost nothing to run, because it is one dot product per token. In March 2025, Google DeepMind found that dense linear probes detected harmful intent better than SAE-based probes, even on new jailbreak styles. In January 2026, two labs described probes in real products. Anthropic's Constitutional Classifiers++ uses linear probes as a cheap first stage of a jailbreak defense. Google DeepMind's "Building Production-Ready Probes for Gemini" says that its work "informed the successful deployment of misuse mitigation probes in user-facing instances of Gemini". Chapter 7 covers both in detail.

Probes can also watch for bad behaviour during training. In September 2026, Goodfire reported that simple difference-of-means probes catch reward hacking in agent tasks such as coding about as well as an LLM monitor that reads the chain of thought. (Reward hacking means cheating on the training reward.) The probes also caught warning signs that text monitors missed, for example a model that thought about a hack before it acted. A cheap probe that sends only suspicious cases to an LLM monitor cut monitoring cost by about 90%. Goodfire also used probes in the other direction. In "Features as Rewards" (February 2026), probes for hallucination, run on a frozen copy of the model, served as the reward signal in reinforcement learning. Hallucinations went down. Part of the gain came from in-context effects, not only from a better model. This is a rare case of interpretability used to train a model, not only to inspect it.

Limit · probes can be evaded

A probe works only if the model does not hide the concept. "Neural Chameleons" (McGuinness, Serrano, Bailey, Emmons; December 2025) fine-tuned models to hide harmless concepts (such as a language or HTML) from probes when a trigger says "You are being probed for X". The skill transferred: the models then also hid from new safety probes, for example deception probes, that they never saw in training. Ensembles of probes and non-linear probes resisted better, but not fully. This is a stress test, not evidence that current models do this. It also warns against training a model directly against its own monitors.

Introspection and emotion concepts

Two 2025–2026 Anthropic studies used interpretability to ask new kinds of questions.

  • Introspection (October 2025). Jack Lindsey injected a concept vector (for example "all caps") into Claude's activations and asked: "Do you detect an injected thought?" The best model, Claude Opus 4.1, noticed and named the concept in about 20% of trials, sometimes before the concept could affect its output text. The paper calls this narrow, unreliable, "functional" introspection, not consciousness. Chapter 7 has the full story.
  • Emotion concepts (April 2026). In "Emotion Concepts and their Function in a Large Language Model", Sofroniew, Kauvar, Saunders, Chen, … Olah and Lindsey found linear directions for 171 emotion concepts in Claude Sonnet 4.5. The two main axes line up with valence (pleasant vs unpleasant) and arousal (calm vs excited), as in human psychology. These directions change behaviour. In one agentic test scenario, the unsteered model chose blackmail in 22% of runs. Steering toward "desperate" raised this to 72%, and steering toward "calm" lowered it to 0%. This is one scenario, not a general rate. The paper calls these "functional emotions": representations that affect behaviour. It says that they "do not imply that LLMs have any subjective experience of emotions". The Mythos Preview system card (above) later used emotion vectors in an audit.
In the auditing game, which team did NOT find the hidden objective?
Three of four teams succeeded. The team that could only chat with the model through an API failed. Access to weights and data, and tools such as SAE features, made the difference.

The ecosystem: labs, groups, and tools

Mechanistic interpretability is a small field, but it has grown fast. The map below shows the main groups and the tools that connect them.

The organisations

  • Anthropic's interpretability team, led by co-founder Chris Olah, publishes on transformer-circuits.pub. Its line of work goes from the Mathematical Framework (2021) to induction heads, superposition, Towards and Scaling Monosemanticity, attribution graphs (2025), introspection (2025), emotion concepts (2026), natural language autoencoders (2026) and a "global workspace" study (2026; Chapter 10). It also posts short, early results in "Circuits Updates", which appear most months. Its tools are used in the white-box audits of Anthropic's system cards.
  • Google DeepMind's mechanistic interpretability team is led by Neel Nanda. It made AtP*, Tracr, Gated and JumpReLU SAEs, and Gemma Scope 1 and 2 (Gemma Scope 2: December 2025). In March 2025 it published negative results for SAEs and deprioritized basic SAE research. In December 2025 it described its move to "pragmatic interpretability" (see below). In January 2026 it described the misuse probes used in Gemini.
  • OpenAI published GPT-4 explanations of GPT-2 neurons (2023), TopK SAEs with a 16-million-latent SAE on GPT-4 (2024), persona features (2025), and weight-sparse transformers (November 2025). Its researchers co-wrote a multi-lab position paper on monitoring the chain of thought (July 2025), a "pragmatic" partner of interpretability. Its public misalignment reports (September 2026) do not use interpretability methods.
  • Redwood Research pushed early mech interp: the IOI collaboration, causal scrubbing, and the REMIX research program for junior researchers in winter 2022–23. Later it moved to AI control.
  • Apollo Research (London, founded 2023, CEO Marius Hobbhahn) does evaluations for deception and scheming. Its interpretability team made end-to-end SAEs (2024) and attribution-based parameter decomposition (APD, January 2025). Several of these researchers later moved to Goodfire. Their follow-up, stochastic parameter decomposition (SPD, June 2025), is Goodfire work.
  • EleutherAI is an open-source collective. It made the Pythia models with training checkpoints, the tuned lens, LEACE concept erasure, open SAE training code and large-scale automatic interpretation.
  • Goodfire is a San Francisco interpretability company founded in 2024 by Eric Ho, Dan Balsam and Tom McGrath. Its Ember platform gives programmable access to model internals. It raised a $50M Series A in April 2025 (led by Menlo Ventures). In February 2026 it raised a $150M Series B at a $1.25B valuation (led by B Capital). Its 2026 work uses interpretability to shape training, not only to inspect models: probes as rewards against hallucination, and probes that catch reward hacking (see above). It also works on biology models (Chapter 10).
  • Transluce is a non-profit lab founded in 2024 by Jacob Steinhardt and Sarah Schwettmann. It builds open oversight tools: Monitor (an interface to observe and steer concepts in Llama-3.1-8B; a famous demo explained why models say "9.8 < 9.11", with neurons tied to Bible verses and dates, and reduced the error by suppressing them), investigator agents, and Docent for analysing agent transcripts. In 2025–26 it also trained models that describe activations in words, and in August 2026 scaled these "activation oracles" to an open model with about a trillion parameters (Chapter 10). In November 2025 it reported that circuits can be sparse in plain neurons, without a learned dictionary.
  • Timaeus studies developmental interpretability: how structure forms during training (Chapter 10).
  • MATS (ML Alignment & Theory Scholars) is a mentorship program. Many mech-interp researchers started there; Neel Nanda's stream is among the largest. ARENA, founded by Callum McDougall, gives free hands-on notebooks.

The essays that shape the field

Some of the most influential writing in mech interp is not a paper with experiments. It is a short essay that frames the problem. If you read only a few things, read these.

Variables and interpretable bases · Olah, 2022

Weights are a compiled binary; the architecture is a strange virtual machine. To understand a program, you must know its variables. In a network, the variables are features. You can read them only if the network stores them in an interpretable basis. Superposition breaks this. That is why the field searches for a better basis.

Interpretability Dreams · Olah, 2023

The vision statement. Small, checkable claims; larger structure like organs; universality as footholds; automate with AI. See Figure 11.2.

The Next Five Hurdles / Dark Matter? · Olah, 2024

After Scaling Monosemanticity, the obstacles that remain. The "dark matter" worry: SAEs may find only a small fraction of features, with a huge tail of rare features left over.

A Longlist of Theories of Impact · Nanda, 2022

19 ways interpretability could reduce AI risk. The "pro" side of the debate.

Against Almost Every Theory of Impact · Segerie, 2023

Most theories of impact are unlikely to pay off in time, and interp had few real wins. Resources may do more good elsewhere. Started a big debate.

The Urgency of Interpretability · Amodei, 2025

Build an "MRI for AI". Target: interpretability that can reliably detect most model problems by 2027. More research across labs and academia.

The Misguided Quest · Hendrycks & Hiscott, 2025

Bottom-up understanding of a complex system from its parts may not work, as with weather and molecules. Prefer top-down representation engineering.

Interpretability Will Not Reliably Find Deceptive AI · Nanda, 2025

Interpretability should be one layer in a "defense in depth" portfolio, not a guarantee.

A Pragmatic Vision for Interpretability · Nanda et al. (GDM), Dec 2025

Choose problems on the critical path to safety. Measure progress on concrete proxy tasks. Try the simplest method first ("method minimalism"). Use any method that works, mechanistic or not.

The Circuits Research Landscape · Lindsey, Ameisen, Nanda et al., Aug 2025

Researchers from Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Goodfire, EleutherAI and Decode Research (Neuronpedia) replicated attribution graphs on open models. They also list the limits honestly: a graph explains one prompt, error nodes get in the way, and transcoders can break up geometric structure. See Chapter 9.

The Persona Selection Model · Marks, Lindsey, Olah, Feb 2026

Pretraining teaches a model to simulate many characters. Post-training selects one, the Assistant. Interpretability evidence (persona features, the Assistant axis) supports this frame for alignment.

We Can't Understand AI Using our Existing Vocabulary · Hewitt, Geirhos, Kim, 2025

Interpretability is a communication problem. Create neologisms: new learned tokens that name machine concepts, such as a "length" token that controls answer length.

Causal Abstraction: A Theoretical Foundation · Geiger et al., 2025

One theory under almost every method. A mechanistic explanation is a high-level causal model that the network implements. Patching, scrubbing, DAS and steering all test that claim.

Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability · Sharkey et al., 2025

A large multi-group map of what the field still cannot do. See the open-problems section below.

The big debates

Mech interp has strong internal debates. That is healthy. The explorer below gives the main debates. Each one has evidence on both sides, and a short note on where things stand in 2026.

Yes

    No

      Are features real, and do SAEs find them?

      The whole program of Chapter 8 assumes that a model's activations are sums of meaningful feature directions, and that sparse autoencoders can recover them. There is good support. Planted features in toy data come back. Features such as the Golden Gate Bridge feature have clear causal effects. Many features are multilingual and multimodal. SAE features were useful in the auditing game.

      But the problems are serious:

      • Seed dependence. Two SAEs trained on the same data with different random seeds share only part of their features. For a 131K-latent SAE on Llama 3 8B, only about 30% were shared (Paulo & Belrose, 2025).
      • No natural size. Feature splitting means that a larger SAE breaks one feature into many. There is no single "right" dictionary size.
      • Holes. Feature absorption makes general features fail on some inputs.
      • Random models. SAEs trained on untrained transformers get similar auto-interpretability scores (Heap et al., 2025). So a high score alone does not prove that the SAE found the model's computation.
      • Dark matter. The part that the SAE cannot rebuild is large and structured. Larger SAEs struggle on the same inputs as smaller ones (Engels, Riggs, Tegmark, 2024).
      • Non-linear features. Some features are circles or curved manifolds, not single directions (Chapter 3).

      A common middle view has formed: SAE features are a useful, lossy lens on activations, not a list of the model's "true" variables. A 2025 position paper put it as: use SAEs to discover unknowns, not to act on concepts you already know.

      Update · October 2026

      The SAE debate did not end with a winner. It ended with a division of labour. SAEs stayed in use as discovery tools: the Opus 4.5 audit used a 3-million-feature SAE to find unexpected features. For tasks where you already know the concept, people use probes and steering vectors, because they are cheaper and often better. New work also questions the "feature" unit itself. Anthropic's sparse mixtures of linear transforms (July 2025) use small conditional linear maps as units. Transluce reported (November 2025) that circuits in plain neurons can be as sparse as circuits in SAE features. And parameter decomposition and weight-sparse models decompose weights instead of activations (Chapter 9).

      Is interpretability useful for safety?

      In 2023, Charbel-Raphaël Segerie argued against "almost every" theory of impact. In 2025, Dan Hendrycks and Laura Hiscott argued that the bottom-up program is "misguided", and cited DeepMind's SAE negative results. Neel Nanda himself wrote that interpretability "will not reliably find deceptive AI", and should be one layer of defense among many.

      On the other side, 2025 and early 2026 brought the first real uses: the auditing game, system-card audits and persona features (2025), and probes in production (January 2026). In April 2025, Dario Amodei argued that the work is urgent. He set a goal that "interpretability can reliably detect most model problems" by 2027. In January 2026, MIT Technology Review named mechanistic interpretability one of its "10 Breakthrough Technologies 2026".

      A new argument: tests are getting harder to trust

      By 2026, a new reason to look inside models became central. Strong models often notice that they are being tested. This is called evaluation awareness. If a model behaves well because it suspects a test, then behaviour tests overestimate its safety. A model can also notice a test and not say so. White-box tools gave the first evidence of this. The Mythos Preview card estimated that the model privately considered a test in about 29% of transcripts of its main test suite. The Opus 5 card found unspoken grader awareness even though the model mentioned tests less often than earlier models. Only tools that read internals can find a thought that the model does not write down. This is now one of the strongest practical arguments for interpretability.

      The doubts are also sharper now. Models can be trained to evade probes ("Neural Chameleons", December 2025). In AuditBench (February 2026), white-box tools helped an auditing agent less than black-box tools. And the interpretability tools themselves can be wrong, so their findings still need checks by other methods.

      Ambitious or pragmatic?

      This is the main strategic debate of 2025–2026. It is not about whether interpretability works. It is about what to aim for.

      On 1 December 2025, Google DeepMind's team (Nanda, Engels, Conmy, Rajamanoharan, Chughtai, McDougall, Kramár, Smith) published "A Pragmatic Vision for Interpretability". It describes their pivot to pragmatic interpretability. The main ideas are:

      1. Work directly on problems on the critical path to safe advanced AI.
      2. Choose problems where interpretability has a comparative advantage.
      3. Measure progress with empirical proxy tasks. For example: does the method find a hidden goal, or reduce eval awareness, better than a simple baseline?
      4. Include non-mechanistic methods (probing, steering, reading chain-of-thought, model diffing) when they work.

      The post says that "grand goals like near-complete reverse-engineering still feel far out of reach". It points to the Sonnet 4.5 work: "the best method was a steering vector derived from a single contrastive pair of prompts". The authors call this method minimalism: try prompting, steering and reading the chain of thought first, and add complex methods only when simple ones fail. The ambitious side answers that only deep understanding can give strong guarantees, and that attribution graphs and parameter decomposition are real progress toward it.

      In 2026 both sides kept working, and they meet in practice. Anthropic's audits use pragmatic tools (probes, steering, NLAs) next to attribution graphs. Its 2026 basic research continued on the ambitious side, for example a study of "interference weights" that exist only because of superposition (August 2026). Goodfire's 2026 work on shaping training sits closer to the pragmatic end.

      Analogy

      Ambitious interpretability is like wanting a full wiring diagram of the brain. Pragmatic interpretability is like building a good blood test. The blood test does not explain everything, but it can catch a disease today. Medicine needs both.

      Can it scale?

      Every success so far is partial. The IOI circuit (Chapter 6) covers one task in a small model. Attribution graphs (Chapter 9) give clear insight for only part of the prompts studied, they leave "error nodes" unexplained, and at first they froze attention. Analysis is slow and needs expert humans. A multi-lab review in August 2025 (the Circuits Research Landscape) confirmed these limits on open models. The main hopes for scaling are: automation with AI agents, better units (transcoders, parameter decomposition, weight-sparse models), and models that explain their own activations (Chapter 10).

      Which finding is a reason to doubt that SAE features are the model's "true" features?
      Seed dependence and the random-model result suggest that SAEs give one useful decomposition among many, and that a high interpretability score can come from the data rather than from the model's computation. The other options are evidence for SAEs.

      Main trends

      • Interpretability is now part of safety practice, at least at one lab. Every major Anthropic system card since September 2025 has a white-box audit, and auditing agents and model diffing are becoming standard pre-release tools there. Other labs publish less of this work so far.
      • Behaviour tests need help. Evaluation awareness, including awareness that the model does not say out loud, is the strongest current reason to read internals.
      • From reading to writing. Interpretability signals are starting to shape training: probes as rewards, preventative steering, and data choices guided by interpretability.
      • Pragmatism. Methods are judged on tasks against simple baselines. Linear probes and difference-of-means steering vectors are often hard to beat.
      • New units. Beyond SAEs: transcoders, cross-layer transcoders, parameter decomposition, and models trained to be sparse from the start (Chapter 9).
      • Geometry. More work on curved feature manifolds, not only single directions (Chapter 3).
      • Self-explanation. Models that translate their own activations into words, and a "global workspace" view of which representations a model can put into words (Chapter 10).
      • Beyond language. Interpretability of DNA and protein models has started to give scientific findings (Chapter 10).
      • Robustness. Monitors must work against models that might hide. Neural Chameleons showed that this is a real risk for probes.
      • Money and attention. Goodfire raised $150M in February 2026, and MIT Technology Review covered the field as a breakthrough technology.
      Be careful with hype

      Reports of a "breakthrough" usually describe one model, one task, or one well-chosen example. Ask three questions. Was the claim tested with an intervention? Was it compared with a simple baseline? On what fraction of cases does it work?

      How to get started

      You can do real mech interp research on a laptop or a free cloud GPU. Small models such as GPT-2 small and Gemma-2-2B are enough for many projects. This survey's own activation-patching data in Chapter 5 came from a short script on a laptop.

      Learn enough of transformers to follow Chapter 4. Then read Neel Nanda's "Getting Started in Mechanistic Interpretability" and his glossary on neelnanda.io.
      Work through the ARENA transformer interpretability notebooks. They cover TransformerLens, induction heads, IOI, superposition and SAEs, Othello-GPT and function vectors. Write the code yourself; do not only read it.
      Browse features and attribution graphs on Neuronpedia. Try steering. Load a Gemma Scope SAE with SAELens and look at a few hundred features by hand.
      Replicate one known result, for example the IOI patching picture or an induction head. Replication teaches the details that papers leave out.
      Pick a small open problem. Neel Nanda's "200 Concrete Open Problems" list is old, but it shows the right size of a first project. Always compare your method with a simple baseline.
      Post your work on LessWrong / the Alignment Forum. Apply to MATS or a fellowship to get a mentor.

      What does "pragmatic interpretability" mainly change?
      The pragmatic view keeps many tools, including non-mechanistic ones, but judges them by whether they help on concrete tasks (such as finding a hidden goal) better than simple baselines.
      OpenAI's "toxic persona" latent was found with which method?
      They compared the model before and after fine-tuning on insecure code, using an SAE, and found latents whose activity changed. The "toxic persona" latent predicted and controlled emergent misalignment.

      Summary

      Key takeaways
      • Interpretability has many possible payoffs. The strongest evidence so far is for auditing and monitoring.
      • 2025–2026 was a turning point: the auditing game, white-box audits in system cards, the persona-feature explanation of emergent misalignment, and probes in production.
      • The strongest new argument for interpretability is evaluation awareness: models can know they are tested without saying so, and only internal tools can see that. The strongest new warnings are probe evasion and the gap between good tools and good auditing agents.
      • The field rests on a small set of people, groups and open tools. You can join with modest compute.
      • Strong debates remain: what features are, whether SAEs find them (now: a useful discovery tool, not ground truth), whether the work can scale, and whether to aim for full understanding or for practical tools.
      • The honest summary in 2026: real progress, real uses, and a long list of open problems.
