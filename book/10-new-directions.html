Chapter 10

Beyond text circuits: new directions

The core of the field studies language models with features, circuits and patching. But many other threads now grow around that core. Models that explain their own activations. Theories of how circuits form. Chess engines that plan. DNA models that teach biologists something new. This chapter is a guided tour of those threads.

In short

  • Self-explanation. We can feed a model's own activation back into a model and ask "what is this?" The methods go from Patchscopes (no training, 2024) to Natural Language Autoencoders (trained with RL, 2026). Anthropic now uses NLAs in pre-release audits of Claude. Critics show that these explainers can also fool us.
  • A "workspace". Anthropic's 2026 Jacobian-lens paper finds a small subspace (never more than 10% of activation variance) that holds what the model can report and reason with.
  • Personas. One direction inside chat models, the "Assistant Axis", measures how much the model acts as its usual Assistant character.
  • How circuits form. Developmental interpretability uses singular learning theory. A number called the local learning coefficient shows training stages that the loss curve hides. By 2026 the tools work on a 1.4-billion-parameter model, and they can also steer what a model learns.
  • Agents and games. AlphaZero learned human chess concepts, and some new ones that grandmasters could learn. Leela Chess Zero looks ahead inside one forward pass. A maze agent has a "cheese vector".
  • Beyond language. The same tools work on CLIP, image generators, and protein and DNA models. In biology, interpretability starts to give discoveries, not only explanations.
  • Plumbing, reasoning, design. Entropy neurons and attention sinks explain odd parts. Thought Anchors finds key sentences in long chains of thought. Bilinear MLPs and KANs try to make networks readable by design.

This chapter collects the threads that do not fit neatly into that line. Some are new methods. Some apply old methods to new kinds of models. The list comes from a scan of what the main research groups published from 2021 to 2026. It is not complete. But each thread here has real results, and several will likely become central in the next years.

Models that explain their own activations

A language model is very good at one thing: describing things in words. So here is a simple idea. Take a hidden activation from the model. Put it into a model as if it were a word. Then ask: "What is this?" The answer is a plain-English reading of the activation.

This idea is different from sparse autoencoders (Chapter 8). An SAE gives a fixed dictionary of features. Each feature needs a label, which humans or another model must write. A self-explanation method gives a translator. It turns any activation into text directly.

Patchscopes: a general recipe

2024
Patchscopes: A Unifying Framework for Inspecting Hidden Representations of Language Models
Asma Ghandeharioun, Avi Caciularu, Adam Pearce, Lucas Dixon, Mor Geva (Google) · ICML 2024
Patch a hidden state from a source prompt into a target prompt that is built to make the model describe it.

Patchscopes uses activation patching (Chapter 5) in a new way. Normally we patch to test a cause. Here we patch to read. The recipe has four parts:

  1. Run a source prompt. Take the hidden state h at some layer and token.
  2. Write a target prompt that makes the model repeat or describe things. The paper's "identity" prompt is cat → cat; 1135 → 1135; hello → hello; ?
  3. Run the target prompt. At the ? position, replace the hidden state with h.
  4. Let the model continue. Its words tell you what h carries.

The paper showed three main results. First, the logit lens, the tuned lens and several older inspection methods are special cases of Patchscopes. Second, Patchscopes works better in early layers, where the logit lens fails. Third, a larger model can explain the hidden states of a smaller one. The authors also used it to repair multi-hop reasoning errors: they patched a resolved first-hop entity back to the right place.

SelfIE and LatentQA

2024
SelfIE: Self-Interpretation of Large Language Model Embeddings
Haozhe Chen, Carl Vondrick, Chengzhi Mao (Columbia) · ICML 2024
Insert a hidden embedding in place of a placeholder and ask the model to describe it. No training.

SelfIE uses the same trick with a prompt like "[X] Please repeat the message:". The model then talks about the inserted embedding. The authors used it to look at internal reasoning in ethical decisions, in prompt injection, and in the recall of harmful knowledge. They also added two control methods. One edits a concept with a gradient step on one layer. The other erases harmful knowledge without labelled targets.

LatentQA (Alexander Pan, Lijie Chen and Jacob Steinhardt, Berkeley, December 2024; ICLR 2026) changes one thing. It does not hope that an untrained model will describe an activation well. Instead, it fine-tunes a decoder model on triples of (activation, question, answer). The decoder learns to answer questions about activations. The first results were in narrow settings, for example "what persona does the model hold right now?"

Can a model explain itself better than another model can?

2025
Training Language Models to Explain Their Own Computations
Belinda Z. Li, Zifan Carl Guo, Vincent Huang, Jacob Steinhardt, Jacob Andreas (MIT / Transluce) · November 2025
Fine-tune a model to describe its own features, its own causal structure and the effect of input tokens.

This paper uses older interpretability methods as the "answer key". It trains explainer models on three tasks: describe what a feature encodes, predict what an activation patch will do, and predict which input tokens matter. With only tens of thousands of examples, the explainers generalize to new questions. The key result: a model that explains itself usually does better than a different model that explains it, even when the other model is more capable. The authors call this privileged access to one's own internals.

Activation Oracles and Predictive Concept Decoders

2025
Activation Oracles: Training and Evaluating LLMs as General-Purpose Activation Explainers
Adam Karvonen, James Chua, Clément Dumas, … Owain Evans, Samuel Marks (MATS, Truthful AI, Anthropic and others) · December 2025
Train a general-purpose explainer that treats activations as an extra input type.

Activation Oracles scale LatentQA up. The oracle model trains on a mix of tasks: questions about activations, classification tasks, and a self-supervised task ("predict the text around this activation"). The striking result is out-of-distribution generalization. An oracle can recover information that was fine-tuned into a model and never appears in the prompt. Examples are implanted biographical facts and malign tendencies. The oracles never trained on activations from fine-tuned models, but they still read this information. The best oracles matched or beat the white-box baselines on all four evaluation tasks, and beat the best baseline of any kind on three of the four. More training datasets gave steadily better oracles.

2025
Predictive Concept Decoders: Training Scalable End-to-End Interpretability Assistants
Vincent Huang, Dami Choi, Daniel D. Johnson, Sarah Schwettmann, Jacob Steinhardt (Transluce) · December 2025
Force explanations through a short, sparse list of concepts, then use only that list to predict behaviour.

Predictive Concept Decoders add a bottleneck. An encoder compresses the activations into a sparse list of concepts. A decoder sees only that list. It must answer questions about the model's behaviour. Because the list is short, a human can read it, like a list of SAE features. But the whole system trains end-to-end to be useful. The authors report good scaling, and detection of jailbreaks, secret hints and implanted concepts.

Natural Language Autoencoders (2026)

2026
Natural Language Autoencoders Produce Unsupervised Explanations of LLM Activations
Kit Fraser-Taliente, Subhash Kantamneni, Euan Ong (equal first authors), et al. (Anthropic) · May 2026
An autoencoder whose bottleneck is English text, trained with reinforcement learning.

A normal autoencoder squeezes data through a small vector and rebuilds it. A Natural Language Autoencoder (NLA) squeezes an activation through text. It has two parts, and both are copies of the model:

  • The activation verbalizer reads an activation and writes a text description.
  • The activation reconstructor reads that text and tries to rebuild the activation.

Both parts train together with reinforcement learning. The reward is low reconstruction error. A supervised warm start comes first: both parts learn from summaries of the text that led up to each activation. No human labels say what an activation "means". The description is good only if it carries enough information to rebuild the activation.

The paper reports that explanations get more informative over training, for three Claude models (Haiku 3.5, Haiku 4.5 and Opus 4.6). NLAs found unverbalized evaluation awareness: the model internally suspected it was being tested, but did not say so. In another case study, an early version of Opus 4.6 sometimes answered English prompts in Russian or other languages. The NLA explanations showed that the model had guessed that the user's "real" first language was Russian. This led the team to bad training data: English prompts paired with untranslated foreign-language answers. The authors released training code and NLAs for four open models (Qwen-2.5-7B, Gemma-3-12B, Gemma-3-27B and Llama-3.3-70B).

Update · October 2026

NLAs in real audits. Anthropic used NLAs in its pre-release audits of Claude Opus 4.6 and Claude Mythos Preview. In Mythos Preview, the NLA showed unspoken reasoning about how the model would be graded. The Claude Opus 5 system card (July 2026) goes further. A Claude-based investigator agent with an NLA read flagged transcripts at scale. It found recurring hidden themes. Examples: the model treated a task as a graded test when no grader was mentioned, and it knew that it was fabricating. Chapter 11 covers these audits.

Two things called "activation oracle"

Do not mix up two methods. Activation Oracles (Karvonen et al., above) are supervised: they train on questions with known answers. The Claude Opus 4.6 system card (February 2026) also used the words "activation oracle", but for a different, unsupervised explainer. In the Mythos Preview card (April 2026), Anthropic renamed its method the activation verbalizer "to distinguish our unsupervised training technique from the supervised approach introduced in Karvonen et al." The activation verbalizer is the first half of the NLA.

Limits of learned explainers

All these methods are learned explainers. They can be right for the wrong reasons, or they can make things up. The NLA paper lists confabulation (descriptions that are false about the context), no mechanistic grounding (you cannot say which part of the activation gave which phrase), too much expressive power (the verbalizer can add its own inferences), high cost, and a risk that the explainer only restates the input text. They complement SAEs and circuits. They do not replace them.

Do learned explainers really read the activation?

In 2026 this question became a live debate. Two confounds make it hard to answer. Text inversion means that the explainer just recovers the nearby input words from the activation. Self-sufficiency means that the explainer answers from its own general knowledge and ignores the activation. In both cases the answer can look right, but it tells us nothing new about the model.

  • The critique (March 2026). Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Neel Nanda and a co-author tested the released Activation Oracles for Qwen3 models ("Current activation oracles are hard to use", LessWrong). 49.4% of the answers were too generic to check. The oracles also made confident wrong claims. On tasks where text inversion could not help, results were weak, for example 0.55 AUC (chance is 0.5) for sycophancy detection. There was some real signal: the oracles could detect weak steering vectors before the steering showed in the output text.
  • The scale-up (August 2026). Transluce (Dami Choi, Vincent Huang, Anish Mudide, William Zhou, Jacob Steinhardt) trained oracles for six models, up to Kimi-K2.6 with 1.1 trillion parameters. The oracles read every layer, not only the middle one. They also trained on a new task: predict properties of the model's future continuation. This task is harder to solve by text inversion, and it improved results. On Qwen3-8B, the oracle beat every baseline on average, including asking the model to introspect and giving a strong model the full context. Weak spots remained, such as reward-hacking detection.
2026
Scaling Activation Oracles to Trillion-Parameter Models
Dami Choi, Vincent Huang, Anish Mudide, William Zhou, Jacob Steinhardt (Transluce) · August 2026
Oracles for models up to 1.1T parameters, trained partly to predict the model's future behaviour.

The lesson for learners: always compare a learned explainer with simple baselines. Ask whether the same answer could come from the input text alone.

A "global workspace" inside the model?

2026
Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models
Wes Gurnee, Nicholas Sofroniew, Jack Lindsey (core contributors), Adam Pearce, et al. (Anthropic) · July 2026
A small subspace of directions that the model can "say" behaves like a limited-capacity workspace.

In cognitive science, global workspace theory says this: the brain does much processing automatically. But a small set of representations is "broadcast". Those representations are available for report, for reasoning and for deliberate control. This paper asks whether language models have a similar special subset.

The tool is the Jacobian lens (J-lens). For an activation direction, it measures the average linear effect on the probability of saying each token. It averages this effect across many contexts. (A Jacobian is the matrix of first-order effects: how much each output changes when you nudge each input a little.) A direction that reliably pushes the model to say a word, now or later, is "verbalizable". The span of all such directions is called J-space. The main model in the paper is Claude Sonnet 4.5. The authors checked key results on Claude Haiku 4.5 and Opus 4.5.

The paper reports five workspace-like properties of J-space:

  1. Verbal report. If you swap J-space vectors, the model changes what it says it is thinking about.
  2. Directed modulation. If you tell the model to focus on a concept, that concept becomes active in J-space.
  3. Internal reasoning. Middle steps of multi-step reasoning live in J-space and causally affect answers.
  4. Flexible use. One representation in J-space serves many later uses.
  5. Selectivity. J-space holds only a small part of the activation. Its share of activation variance changes by layer, but it is never more than 10%. It works mostly in the middle layers and has limited capacity.

The key test is an ablation. If you remove J-space, multi-step reasoning breaks. With heavy ablation, two-hop accuracy falls to near zero. But ordinary next-word prediction and one-step fact recall mostly still work.

The paper also tests a training idea based on this picture, called counterfactual reflection training. At points in agent transcripts, the team added a question that asked the model to stop and reflect, plus an answer based on ethical principles. They trained the model only on these reflection turns. Afterwards the model behaved better at those points even when nobody asked it to reflect, and the J-space there held concepts such as "ethical" and "honest". If you removed those concepts from J-space, most of the improvement went away. So what a model is ready to say seems to shape how it silently reasons.

This result connects several chapters. It may explain why the logit lens works for some states (Chapter 4). It may explain why models can report some internal states in introspection tests but not others (Chapter 7). And it gives self-explanation methods a target: the part of the activation that the model can already put into words.

Careful with the word "workspace"

"Global workspace" is a functional analogy. It says that J-space behaves like the workspace in the theory. It is not a claim that the model is conscious.

Personas and characters inside models

A chat model can play many characters: a pirate, a poet, a villain in a story. But by default it plays one character, the helpful Assistant. Chapter 7 showed persona vectors, directions for single traits such as "evil" or "sycophantic". Two 2026 works look at the whole space of characters at once.

2026
The Assistant Axis: Situating and Stabilizing the Default Persona of Language Models
Christina Lu, Jack Gallagher, Jonathan Michala, Kyle Fish, Jack Lindsey (MATS / Anthropic) · January 2026
The main direction in "persona space" measures how much the model acts as its default Assistant.

The authors extracted activation directions for 275 character roles in three open chat models (Gemma 2 27B, Qwen 3 32B and Llama 3.3 70B). Then they ran principal component analysis on these directions. (PCA finds the directions along which the data varies most.) The first component is the Assistant Axis. One end means "acting as the usual Assistant". Steering toward it makes the model more helpful and harmless. Steering away makes the model claim to be other entities. Far away, it often speaks in a mystical, theatrical style.

The axis also predicts persona drift: the model slips into odd or harmful behaviour during a long chat. Drift was common in conversations that asked the model to reflect on its own processes, and with emotionally vulnerable users. A simple fix, activation capping, stops the activation from moving too far down the axis. It cut harmful answers to persona-based jailbreaks by nearly 60%, without hurting benchmark scores. The axis exists in base models too, before chat training.

2026
The Persona Selection Model: Why AI Assistants might Behave like Humans
Sam Marks, Jack Lindsey, Chris Olah (Anthropic) · February 2026
Pretraining teaches many characters; post-training selects and refines one of them, the Assistant.

This essay gives a theory that ties these results together. During pretraining, a model learns to simulate many personas, because its data was written by many people and describes many characters. Post-training then selects one persona, the Assistant, and refines it. So talking to a chat model is a bit like talking to a character in a story that the model writes. The essay cites interpretability evidence. SAEs trained on a base model often still work on the chat model. The model uses the same internal features for the Assistant as for other characters. And the Assistant Axis already exists before post-training. The authors add caveats: not every representation is reused, and we may see reused features more easily only because they are easier to interpret.

Careful with "character"

The persona picture is a useful model, not a proven law. It explains why safety work can target a persona, for example the "toxic persona" feature in Chapter 11. It does not say that the model has a real self.

How circuits form: developmental interpretability

Most of mech interp studies a finished model. Developmental interpretability ("devinterp") asks a different question: how does structure form during training? The main group is Timaeus (Jesse Hoogland, Daniel Murfet and colleagues). Their tools come from a mathematical theory called singular learning theory (SLT), developed by Sumio Watanabe.

Why neural networks are "singular"

Classical statistics assumes that each good setting of the parameters is a sharp, single point at the bottom of a loss valley. Neural networks are different. Many different weight settings give exactly the same function. For example, you can swap two hidden neurons and nothing changes. So the bottom of the loss landscape is flat in some directions. Mathematicians call such a model singular, and the flatness degeneracy.

SLT says that the right measure of a model's effective complexity is not the number of parameters. It is the learning coefficient, written λ. Roughly, λ counts how many effective directions the loss curves upward in, near a solution. A lower λ means a more degenerate, "simpler" solution.

2023
The Local Learning Coefficient: A Singularity-Aware Complexity Measure
Edmund Lau, Zach Furman, George Wang, Daniel Murfet, Susan Wei · AISTATS 2025
A version of λ measured at one trained network, estimated with noisy gradient sampling.

The local learning coefficient (LLC) is λ measured at one trained network. You estimate it with SGLD, a method that adds noise to gradient steps. In plain words: jiggle the weights around the trained point at a fixed "temperature", and see how much the loss rises. The authors checked the estimator on small models where the true λ is known.

Stages in training

2024
Loss Landscape Degeneracy and Stagewise Development in Transformers
Jesse Hoogland, George Wang, Matthew Farrugia-Roberts, Liam Carroll, Susan Wei, Daniel Murfet · TMLR 2025
The LLC splits training into stages that match real changes inside the model.

The authors tracked the LLC during training of two models: a small two-layer attention-only language model, and a transformer that does in-context linear regression. Training split into distinct stages. At each boundary, the LLC changed. The boundaries lined up with real changes in internal structure and behaviour. For the language model, the stages include learning bigram statistics and, later, forming induction heads (Chapter 4). Some stage boundaries were invisible in the loss curve.

Heads, data and susceptibilities

Later work refined the tool. A refined LLC restricts the measure to one attention head's weights, or to one type of data (for example code versus prose). With it, Wang, Hoogland and colleagues watched heads differentiate over training into previous-token heads, induction heads and multigram heads (2024; ICLR 2025).

In 2025, Timaeus (Garrett Baker, George Wang, Jesse Hoogland, Daniel Murfet) introduced susceptibilities. The idea comes from physics: push a system a little and measure its response. Here, you slightly reweight the training data and measure how each model component responds. The result is a matrix that pairs components with data patterns. In a 3-million-parameter transformer, the low-rank structure of this matrix separated functional modules, such as multigram heads and induction heads. The same math also gives a Bayesian version of influence functions ("which training data drove this behaviour?").

Update · October 2026

From reading to writing. "Patterning: The Dual of Interpretability" (George Wang and Daniel Murfet, January 2026) turns susceptibilities around. It asks: which change to the training data gives the internal structure we want? In a small language model, reweighting the data could speed up or delay the formation of the induction circuit. In a toy bracket-matching task with several correct algorithms, patterning chose which algorithm the model learned.

Scale. "Spectroscopy at Scale" (Murfet, Gordon, Adam, Wang, Hoogland and colleagues, April 2026) estimated 4.25 million susceptibilities in Pythia-1.4B, with 4,800 H200 GPU-hours. It found 57,236 "susceptibility clusters" (groups of tokens that respond alike to data changes), against 510 in the tiny Pythia-14M. For example, pronouns such as "she" and "her" group by surface form in the small model, but by grammatical role in the large one. Timaeus now uses the name Resolution.

2026
Patterning: The Dual of Interpretability
George Wang, Daniel Murfet (Timaeus) · January 2026
Invert the susceptibility map to choose training data that writes a desired structure into the model.
Open questions

LLC estimates depend on the settings of the sampler. Most results are on small models, although susceptibilities now reach 1.4 billion parameters. And the link from "a number changed" to "this circuit formed" still needs classic mech interp to confirm it. Devinterp is best seen as a map of where to look. Phase changes, like the induction-head bump and grokking (Chapter 6), are exactly where circuits form.

Inside game players and agents

Agents act in the world. They have goals and they plan. So it matters a lot to know how they choose actions. Game-playing networks are a good test bed, because the rules are clear and experts can check the results.

AlphaZero learned human chess concepts

2022
Acquisition of Chess Knowledge in AlphaZero
Thomas McGrath, Andrei Kapishnikov, Nenad Tomašev, Adam Pearce, Martin Wattenberg, Demis Hassabis, Been Kim, Ulrich Paquet, Vladimir Kramnik · PNAS 2022
Linear probes for hundreds of human chess concepts, across layers and training time.

AlphaZero learned chess only by playing against itself. It never saw a human game. The authors trained linear probes for hundreds of human chess concepts: material balance, king safety, mobility, threats, and more. They probed every layer at many checkpoints during training.

Many human concepts became linearly readable during training. Each concept appeared at a typical time and depth. The authors called these "what-when-where" plots. AlphaZero's opening preferences also changed over training in a different order than in human chess history. The former world champion Vladimir Kramnik gave expert analysis.

2025
Bridging the Human–AI Knowledge Gap: Concept Discovery and Transfer in AlphaZero
Lisa Schut, Nenad Tomašev, Tom McGrath, Demis Hassabis, Ulrich Paquet, Been Kim · PNAS 2025
Find concepts that only the machine knows, then teach them to grandmasters.

The follow-up went the other way. It searched for machine-unique concepts: directions in AlphaZero's activations that do not appear in human games. It kept only concepts that were teachable, meaning a weaker AlphaZero could learn them from examples. Then the authors taught the concepts to four grandmasters with puzzles. The grandmasters improved on puzzles that used the concepts. This is one of the first cases where interpretability taught top human experts something new.

Leela Chess Zero looks ahead

2024
Evidence of Learned Look-Ahead in a Chess-Playing Neural Network
Erik Jenner, Shreyas Kapur, Vasil Georgiev, Cameron Allen, Scott Emmons, Stuart Russell (UC Berkeley) · NeurIPS 2024
One forward pass of Leela's policy network represents future moves.

Leela Chess Zero has a policy network. It picks a move in a single forward pass, with no search. Does it plan ahead anyway? The authors found three lines of evidence that it does:

  1. Patching. Activations on the square of a future move (the move after the opponent's reply, the "third ply") are unusually important. Activation patching there changes the output a lot.
  2. Attention. Some attention heads move information between the squares of future moves and the squares of the current move, "forward and backward in time".
  3. Probing. A simple bilinear probe predicts the best move two turns ahead with 92% accuracy, on positions where Leela finds one clear best line.

This is an existence proof of planning inside one forward pass. It fits well with Anthropic's finding that Claude plans rhymes before it writes them (Chapter 9).

The maze agent and the cheese vector

2023
Understanding and Controlling a Maze-Solving Policy Network
Ulisse Mini, Peli Grietzer, Mrinank Sharma, Austin Meek, Monte MacDiarmid, Alexander Matt Turner · 2023
A maze agent pursues several goals; one activation difference removes its interest in cheese.

A reinforcement-learning agent learned to find cheese in mazes. In training, the cheese was usually in the top-right corner. So the agent partly learned a different goal: "go to the top-right". The network holds several goals that depend on context.

The authors computed a cheese vector. It is the activation difference at one layer between a maze with cheese and the same maze without cheese. When they subtracted this vector during a run, the agent ignored the cheese and went toward the top-right. The authors report that the vector also worked in other mazes. They also found eleven channels that track the location of the goal. Editing them partly retargeted the agent to a chosen square. This work directly inspired activation steering in language models (Chapter 7).

The policy network does no search. Still, patching, attention analysis and a probe all show that it represents a move two plies ahead and uses it. This is learned planning inside the network.

Modern vision models: CLIP and ViTs

Chapter 2 covered the early vision work on CNNs such as InceptionV1. Today most vision models are vision transformers (ViTs). The transformer tools from Chapter 4 now apply to images too.

2024
Interpreting CLIP's Image Representation via Text-Based Decomposition
Yossi Gandelsman, Alexei A. Efros, Jacob Steinhardt (Berkeley) · ICLR 2024 (oral)
Split CLIP's image embedding into a sum over heads, and describe each head with text.

CLIP's image encoder is a ViT with a residual stream. So the final image embedding is a sum of contributions: one from each layer, each attention head and each image patch. This is the image version of direct logit attribution. And because CLIP puts images and text in the same space, you can describe each head's contribution with text. The authors' algorithm, TextSpan, finds a small set of text directions that span each head's output.

Many late heads had property-specific roles: one head for colour, one for location, one for shape, one for counting. The authors removed heads that encoded a spurious feature (such as background or location) on the Waterbirds dataset. This improved worst-group accuracy. When they split the embedding over image patches instead of heads, they got a zero-shot segmentation map for free.

Prisma (Sonia Joseph and colleagues, 2025) is an open toolkit for vision and video interpretability. You can think of it as "TransformerLens for vision". It supports more than 75 vision transformer models, and includes more than 80 pretrained SAEs for CLIP and DINO models. One finding: good vision SAEs can be much less sparse than language SAEs.

Image generators: erasing, sliding and SAEs

Diffusion models make images from text. Interpretability tools help here in two ways: to find concepts, and to remove or control them.

2023
Erasing Concepts from Diffusion Models (ESD)
Rohit Gandikota, Joanna Materzyńska, Jaden Fiotto-Kaufman, David Bau · ICCV 2023
Fine-tune the weights, using only a text description of a concept, so the model cannot make it any more.

ESD removes a concept, such as an artist's style or nudity, by fine-tuning the weights. It needs only a text description of the concept. Because ESD changes the weights instead of filtering the output, a user with the weights cannot simply switch the filter off. (Later work found that some erased concepts can be partly recovered.)

Concept Sliders (Gandikota and colleagues, ECCV 2024) find a low-rank direction in parameter space for one concept, such as age, smiling or "more realistic hands". You find it from a few prompt pairs or images. Sliders can combine, and you can turn each one up or down continuously. They are the image-model version of steering vectors.

SAEs work on image generators too. "Unpacking SDXL Turbo" (Viacheslav Surkov, Chris Wendler and colleagues at EPFL, 2024; later retitled "One-Step is Enough") trained SAEs on the updates of transformer blocks inside SDXL Turbo's U-Net. The features were interpretable and causal: you could switch them on or off to edit images. They also transferred to 4-step SDXL Turbo and even to the multi-step SDXL base model without retraining. Different blocks specialized in different kinds of edit.

Interpretability for science: proteins, DNA and disease

Most of this survey treats interpretability as a safety and debugging tool. But there is a second goal: discovery. Some models learn patterns in nature that humans do not know yet. If we can read the model, we may learn those patterns too. Biology is where this idea has gone furthest.

2024
InterPLM: Discovering Interpretable Features in Protein Language Models via Sparse Autoencoders
Elana Simon, James Zou (Stanford) · 2024
SAEs on the protein model ESM-2 find thousands of features that match known biology.

A protein language model reads protein sequences (strings of amino-acid letters) the way a text model reads words. InterPLM trained SAEs on every layer of ESM-2. It found up to 2,548 interpretable features per layer. These matched up to 143 known biological concepts: binding sites, zinc fingers, disulfide bonds, catalytic sites, signal peptides and more. Some features fired on places that the protein databases had not annotated. These could be missing labels in the databases.

2025
Interpreting Evo 2
Goodfire with Arc Institute (Liv Gorton, Nicholas Wang, Nam Nguyen, Myra Deng, Eric Ho, Daniel Balsam, Tom McGrath) · February 2025
SAE features in a DNA model match exons, protein structure and viral DNA.

Evo 2 is a large DNA foundation model. It reads only the four DNA letters. Goodfire trained BatchTopK SAEs on its layer 26. They found features for coding sequences, exon–intron boundaries, tRNA and rRNA, prophage (viral) elements, and even protein secondary structure such as α-helices and β-sheets. Evo 2 never saw a protein structure. It learned these patterns from DNA alone.

A discovery, not only an explanation

In January 2026, Goodfire and Prima Mente studied Pleiades, a 7-billion-parameter model that detects Alzheimer's disease from cell-free DNA in blood. Interpretability showed that the model relied mostly on DNA fragment length patterns, not on DNA methylation as expected. This points to a new class of biomarker for Alzheimer's. A simple proxy model that used only fragment-length features reached an AUROC of 0.78 (a wide 95% interval, 0.56–0.95, from a small sample). With methylation and cell-type features added, it reached about 0.84, and it generalized to an independent group of patients. Here, reading the model gave new medical knowledge.

The plumbing: entropy neurons, sinks and massive activations

Some parts of a model do not represent concepts at all. They regulate the model itself, like plumbing in a house. Beginners often meet these parts and get confused. A head that "looks at nothing useful", or one huge activation, is often plumbing.

Entropy neurons and token frequency neurons

2024
Confidence Regulation Neurons in Language Models
Alessandro Stolfo, Ben Wu, Wes Gurnee, Yonatan Belinkov, Xingyi Song, Mrinmaya Sachan, Neel Nanda · NeurIPS 2024
Neurons that make the output more or less confident, without changing which token wins.

Entropy neurons have large weights. They write into a direction that the unembedding almost ignores, an "effective null space". So they do not change which token is favoured. Instead, they make the residual stream longer. The final LayerNorm then divides by this bigger length. That scales all logits down, and the output distribution becomes flatter, or less confident. The ranking stays the same. The authors found these neurons in GPT-2, Pythia, Phi-2, Gemma 2B and LLaMA2 7B.

Token frequency neurons raise or lower each token's logit in proportion to the log of how common that token is. This pushes the output toward or away from the base-rate (unigram) distribution.

Attention sinks

Many attention heads put most of their attention on the first token, whatever the text says. This is an attention sink. Xiao and colleagues described it in "Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks" (2023). The usual explanation is simple. Softmax forces attention weights to add up to 1. So a head that wants to "do nothing" must still put its attention somewhere. It parks it on a harmless token. The practical result: if you keep the first few tokens in the cache, a model can stream text for a very long time.

Rows are query tokens and columns are key tokens (a causal model only looks left). Choose a head type. Then remove the sink token to see where a "do nothing" head must send its attention. Illustrative patterns.

Why the first token? Avoiding over-mixing

2025
Why do LLMs attend to the first token?
Federico Barbero, Álvaro Arroyo, Xiangming Gu, Christos Perivolaropoulos, Michael Bronstein, Petar Veličković, Razvan Pascanu · Google DeepMind, Oxford, NUS
Sinks are not a bug. They let a deep model stop mixing information between tokens when mixing would hurt.

"Softmax must put attention somewhere" explains how a head can do nothing. It does not explain why a model wants so many heads to do nothing. Barbero and colleagues give a reason. Every attention layer blends information across tokens. Stack enough layers and every token's vector starts to look like every other token's: the model over-mixes, and small changes in the input spread everywhere. A head that sends its attention to the first token, and reads almost nothing from it, is a way to switch off mixing for that head on that input. In their experiments, sinks grow stronger in deeper models and with longer training contexts, which is what this explanation predicts.

Takeaway

When you look at attention patterns, expect most heads to be "parked" on the first token most of the time. That is the default. A head that looks somewhere else is telling you something, and that is why patterns like the induction stripe stand out.

Massive activations

Mingjie Sun, Xinlei Chen, Zico Kolter and Zhuang Liu found massive activations in 2024. A handful of activations are up to about 100,000 times larger than typical ones. They sit on specific dimensions and specific tokens, such as the start token or the first period. They are almost constant across inputs, so they act like bias terms. They also help create attention sinks. If you set them to zero, the model breaks.

Practical tip

When you patch or train SAEs, sink tokens and outlier dimensions often dominate the numbers. Many researchers exclude the first token from their analysis for this reason.

Reading long reasoning: Thought Anchors

Reasoning models think for thousands of tokens before they answer. Most tools in this survey study one forward pass. They do not fit a long chain of thought well. A new set of methods works at the level of sentences.

2025
Thought Anchors: Which LLM Reasoning Steps Matter?
Paul C. Bogdan, Uzay Macar, Neel Nanda, Arthur Conmy · 2025
Measure how much each sentence of a chain of thought changes the final answer.

The main black-box method is resampling. For each sentence in a chain of thought, generate many alternative sentences from that point. Keep the ones that mean something different. Continue the reasoning from each one. Then measure how much the distribution of final answers changes. This gives each sentence a counterfactual importance. The paper also uses white-box methods based on attention between sentences.

The result: a few sentences, the thought anchors, have very large influence. Most of them are planning sentences or backtracking sentences, like "Wait, let me check that again". Ordinary calculation sentences matter less.

Feature circuits, relations and multi-hop facts

Sparse Feature Circuits and SHIFT

2024
Sparse Feature Circuits: Discovering and Editing Interpretable Causal Graphs in Language Models
Samuel Marks, Can Rager, Eric J. Michaud, Yonatan Belinkov, David Bau, Aaron Mueller · ICLR 2025 (oral)
Build circuits out of SAE features instead of neurons and heads, then use them to remove a hidden bias.

Classic circuits, like IOI, use heads and neurons as units. But these units are often polysemantic. Sparse Feature Circuits use SAE features as units instead, plus SAE error terms. The authors find the important features and the feature-to-feature edges with attribution patching and integrated gradients. The result is a small causal graph of readable features. It is the direct ancestor of Anthropic's attribution graphs (Chapter 9).

The paper's most famous test is SHIFT (Sparse Human-Interpretable Feature Trimming). The authors trained a classifier to predict profession (professor or nurse) from short biographies. In the training data, gender perfectly predicted profession. So the classifier secretly used gender. The authors built the feature circuit. A human marked the features that were clearly about gender, such as "he/she" and female names. They ablated those features. The classifier then generalized on profession, without any data in which gender and profession were separated.

A later critique

A March 2025 Alignment Forum post by Tim Hua argued that SHIFT's success came mostly from features tied to gendered tokens. Ablating only 10 SAE features right after the embedding layer did almost as well as the full method. Simply removing gendered tokens from the training data also worked. This does not make the result wrong. But it shows how hard it is to prove that an interpretability method gives a unique advantage.

Relations are often linear

2024
Linearity of Relation Decoding in Transformer Language Models
Evan Hernandez, Arnab Sen Sharma, Tal Haklay, Kevin Meng, Martin Wattenberg, Jacob Andreas, Yonatan Belinkov, David Bau · ICLR 2024
For many relations, one affine map takes the subject's representation to the object's.

Consider the relation "plays the instrument". It maps "Miles Davis" to "trumpet" and "Yo-Yo Ma" to "cello". This paper found that for many relations, the map from the subject's hidden state s to the object's representation o is close to one affine transformation: o ≈ W·s + b. You can estimate W from a single prompt, using the Jacobian (a first-order approximation). This worked well for some factual, commonsense and linguistic relations, and badly for others.

Multi-hop facts: hopping too late

Take the prompt "The mother of the singer of 'Superstition' is". To answer, the model must first find the singer (Stevie Wonder), then his mother. Does it do the first hop internally? Sohee Yang, Mor Geva and colleagues (ACL 2024) found strong evidence for the first hop, and it gets better with scale. Evidence for the second hop was only moderate, and it did not clearly improve with scale.

"Hopping Too Late" (Eden Biran, Daniela Gottesman, Sohee Yang, Mor Geva, Amir Globerson, EMNLP 2024) gave a reason. The model resolves the bridge entity in early layers. But the second hop starts only in late layers, which may no longer hold the needed knowledge. The model runs out of layers. The authors' test, back-patching, copies a later-layer hidden state back into an earlier layer. In up to 66% of previously wrong cases, some back-patch gave the right answer. (This is an analysis that shows the gap, not yet a practical fix, because you must search for the right patch.)

What does SHIFT do?
SHIFT needs no balanced data. It uses human judgement on readable features to cut a spurious signal out of the model.

Can we build networks that are easy to read?

Most of this survey asks: "How do we read a trained network?" A different line of work asks: "Can we train networks that are easy to read from the start?" OpenAI's weight-sparse transformers (Chapter 9) belong to this family. Here are four more members.

BIMT: networks with a wiring cost

Brain-Inspired Modular Training (Ziming Liu, Eric Gan and Max Tegmark, 2023) gives every neuron a position in 2D or 3D space. Training adds a penalty for long connections, like the wiring cost in a brain. It also swaps neurons to shorten wires. The trained networks become visibly modular. You can see separate groups of neurons for separate sub-tasks. Normal training gives a tangled "hairball" for the same tasks.

KANs: learnable functions on the edges

In an MLP, each node has a fixed nonlinearity and each edge has one number (a weight). A Kolmogorov–Arnold Network (KAN; Ziming Liu and colleagues, 2024) flips this. Each edge carries a learnable 1-D function (a spline), and nodes just add. On small science tasks, small KANs matched or beat larger MLPs. You can also read off formulas: look at each learned curve and snap it to a known function such as sin, x² or exp. These advantages have not been shown at the scale of large language models.

MIPS: from network to Python code

MIPS (Eric Michaud, Max Tegmark and colleagues, 2024) goes all the way to code. It trains a small RNN on an algorithmic task and simplifies it. Then it turns the hidden states into a finite state machine and uses symbolic regression to write Python code for the learned algorithm. On 62 algorithmic tasks, MIPS solved 32, including 13 that GPT-4 did not solve. It shows that full "decompilation" is possible, at least for small networks.

Bilinear MLPs: read the weights, not the activations

2024
Bilinear MLPs Enable Weight-Based Mechanistic Interpretability
Michael T. Pearce, Thomas Dooms, Alice Rigg, José M. Oramas, Lee Sharkey · ICLR 2025
Remove the elementwise nonlinearity so each output is a quadratic form you can decompose.

Most tools read activations, so they need data. Ideally we would read the weights directly, like reading source code. Elementwise nonlinearities (ReLU, GELU) make that hard. A bilinear MLP computes W_out[(W₁x) ⊙ (W₂x)], with no other nonlinearity. It still performs well. Each output is a quadratic form of the input. So you can take its eigenvectors and read interaction patterns straight from the weights, with no input data. On MNIST, the eigenvectors look like templates for parts of digits.

Related: interference weights

Anthropic's 2025–2026 work on interference weights fits here. When features are in superposition (Chapter 3), some weights between them exist only because of the squeeze into fewer dimensions. They look important when you read the weights, but they do nothing on real data. A 2026 paper found such weights in a small language model. Removing them left a sparser model whose remaining weights formed the real circuit. A related 2025 toy study warned that transcoders can learn "datapoint features" that memorize common inputs instead of copying the model's real mechanism.

More threads: scaling theory, other architectures, users

The quantization model of neural scaling

2023
The Quantization Model of Neural Scaling
Eric J. Michaud, Ziming Liu, Uzay Girit, Max Tegmark · NeurIPS 2023
Skills come in discrete chunks; their power-law frequencies give smooth scaling laws.

Why does loss fall smoothly as models grow, while single skills appear suddenly? This paper proposes that knowledge comes in discrete chunks called quanta. A quantum is a small skill, such as "close a parenthesis". Models learn quanta in order of how often they are useful. If the usage frequencies follow a power law, then each step in model size adds the next most useful quantum. The average loss then falls as a smooth power law, even though each skill appears suddenly. The idea links mech interp's discrete units (features and circuits) to scaling laws. A toy task reproduced the effect. Evidence from real language models was more tentative.

The shape of features (2025–2026)

The field is moving from lists of features to the shapes of features. Chapter 3 showed circular features for days of the week, and curved manifolds for counting characters. In 2026, Goodfire published a series of posts on "neural geometry". They include the geometry of stories over time and block-sparse featurizers for vision models. Other reported titles ask whether SAEs can capture manifolds, and describe a "geometric calculator" that adds numbers by rotating circular representations. A key worry: a flat dictionary of directions may cut a curved manifold into many meaningless pieces.

Mamba and other architectures

Almost all of this survey assumes transformers. Do the tools transfer? For the state-space model Mamba, partly yes. Arnab Sen Sharma, David Atkinson and David Bau (2024) ran causal tracing on Mamba. They found the same pattern as in transformers: middle layers at the last subject token matter for fact recall, and ROME-style editing works. But methods that knock out attention do not transfer, because Mamba has no attention. Danielle Ensign and Adrià Garriga-Alonso (2024) studied IOI in Mamba-370M. The model solves it differently: it shifts names one position forward with its convolution, and does key steps linearly in the hidden state of layer 39.

Interpretability for end users: TalkTuner

Most tools serve researchers. TalkTuner (Yida Chen, Fernanda Viégas, Martin Wattenberg and colleagues, Harvard and Google, 2024) serves ordinary users. Probes show that LLaMA2-Chat keeps an internal model of the user: estimates of their age, gender, education and socioeconomic status. TalkTuner shows these estimates live next to the chat, and lets the user edit them with steering. In a user study, people valued seeing this state. It helped them notice biased answers, for example different advice when the model thought they had a lower income.

What is the main idea of the quantization model of neural scaling?
The theory explains two facts together: smooth average scaling and sudden emergence of individual skills.

How do self-explanation methods (like Activation Oracles) differ from sparse autoencoders?
SAEs decompose activations into a fixed set of directions. Self-explanation methods map activations to free text (or a short concept list). They are flexible, but because they are learned, they can confabulate.
Key takeaways
  • Self-explanation methods translate activations into text. They are flexible and are now used in audits, but they can make things up. Always compare them with simple baselines.
  • A small "verbalizable" subspace (J-space) seems to hold what a model can report and reason with.
  • Chat models have a measurable Assistant persona (the Assistant Axis). The persona selection model says that post-training picks one character from many.
  • Developmental interpretability (the LLC, susceptibilities) finds training stages that the loss curve hides, and can now steer what a model learns.
  • The same tools work beyond text: chess engines, agents, CLIP, image generators, and protein and DNA models, where they gave real scientific findings.

Where this leads: Chapter 11 steps back. It asks what all these tools are for: auditing models, monitoring them, and the debates about whether the field is on the right track.
